About
Plainly is AI news for people with other things to do.
I started it in the spring of 2026 while in law school, because the AI stories that mattered most to my own life — the ones about rent, jobs, courts, and contracts — were being written for engineers. This is my attempt to write them for everyone else.
Ryan Mitchell, editor  ·  Los Angeles
How this started

I started using AI to get through law school, and then started reading about it.

In my second year I began using AI tools the way most students eventually do: to summarize a reading I didn’t have time for, to quiz myself before an exam, to turn a pile of notes into an outline. It worked better than I expected, and that made me curious about what else it could do and where it was going.

So I read. Most of what I found assumed I already worked in technology. It used terms it didn’t define, skipped the part about why any of it mattered, and was written for people who had been following the field for years. The information was out there. It just wasn’t written for most people.

The stories I actually wanted were simpler than that. Not how the models work, but what happens when a landlord’s software sets your rent, when a judge fines a lawyer for citing cases that don’t exist, when the government orders a product offline that millions of people used that morning. Those are AI stories, and they belong to everyone.

What Plainly is

A news site that covers AI by what it does to ordinary life, not by how it works.

Plainly does two things. It reports on AI the way a newspaper reports on anything else: what happened, who it affects, and what you can do about it. Each story is filed under the part of life it touches — law, business, real estate, careers, academia, regulation, sports, the arts — because that is how people actually run into it.

It also tries to make the tools themselves less intimidating. Most people have never been shown how to get anything useful out of them. The Framework is a short method for finding out what AI can take off your plate. The prompts library is a set of starting points you can copy and use today. Neither requires technical knowledge.

A few rules hold throughout. Every term gets explained the first time it appears. When something is uncertain, the story says so. When a company is the only source for a claim, the story says that too. And a story doesn’t run unless it tells you something you can use.

Who writes it

One person, for now.

I’m a law student in Los Angeles. Every story is researched, drafted, and fact-checked with help from AI tools, then read and edited by me before it runs. Where that matters, the story says so — including a disclosure whenever Anthropic, whose models I use, is the subject. I’m not an expert in artificial intelligence. I’m a careful reader who tries to write things down clearly, and that turns out to be most of the job.

If I’ve gotten something wrong, I’d like to know. If you have a story, I’d like to hear it.

The editorial principles
What Plainly stands for
1
Plain English Every term is explained the first time it appears. If a sentence needs a background in technology to follow, it gets rewritten.
2
Honest about uncertainty When something isn’t known, the story says so. When a company is the only source, the story says that too.
3
Filed by the part of life it touches Stories are sorted by sector — law, housing, work, school — not by which company announced what.
4
No hype, no doom Both are easier to write than an accurate account. Plainly reports what happened and lets you decide what it means.
5
Useful A story doesn’t run unless it tells you something you can act on, even if the action is just knowing.
